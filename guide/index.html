How an AMOLED Display Forms an Image

An AMOLED (active-matrix organic light-emitting diode) panel has no backlight. Every subpixel is its own organic light-emitting diode: apply a drive current and it emits light directly; cut the current and it goes dark. An image is not a filtered white lamp — it is thousands of tiny independent lamps switched on and off by a per-pixel backplane, in strict time with a refresh cycle.

Between the emitters sits the black matrix: opaque gaps that stop light from one subpixel washing into the next. What you perceive as "black" on an OLED is the absence of emission in that region, which is why true black reads infinitely deeper than an LCD's backlit gray. What you perceive as a smooth color field is your visual system spatially averaging discrete red, green and blue emitters over the panel's subpixel geometry.

That geometry is the whole story. Two panels receiving identical RGB data will draw different pictures at the subpixel level, because the arrangement and count of their emitters differ. A Diamond PenTile AMOLED simulator exists precisely to make that difference visible — instead of showing a downscaled screenshot of a screen, it reconstructs the emitter lattice itself.

Diamond PenTile vs RGB Stripe: The Density Math

The RGB stripe pixel

The familiar LCD / LED-monitor arrangement: each logical pixel owns three equal rectangular subpixels in a fixed vertical stripe — one red, one green, one blue. Every channel is sampled at full horizontal resolution, subpixel columns line up in straight runs down the panel, and the rendering contract is simple: one pixel in, three rectangles out.

The Diamond PenTile lattice

Diamond PenTile (the layout used across most modern phone AMOLED panels) breaks that contract. Subpixels sit on a staggered, diamond-tiled lattice:

  • Green emitters are small circles packed in a dense checker — twice as many as any other channel, because green carries most of perceived luminance.
  • Red and blue emitters are larger diamonds interleaved at half the green density, alternating sides on adjacent rows.
  • Neighbouring pixels share subpixels — there is no private three-rectangle box you can point at.

Count it over a two-pixel pair and you get roughly 2 green + 1 red + 1 blue emitters per pixel — about two subpixels per pixel instead of three. Each chroma channel therefore resolves at half horizontal resolution, while green keeps a high-resolution sampling grid. The panel trades chroma sampling density for luminance fidelity and emitter lifetime — the same trade a simulator lets you inspect directly, magnification by magnification.

This is the difference a screen matrix test exposes instantly: feed both lattices the same hard edge and the PenTile grid breaks it into a staggered staircase of individual emitters where the stripe grid draws a clean column.

Chromatic Fringing & Subpixel Rendering Algorithms

Because red and blue sample at half horizontal resolution, a sharp vertical edge written straight to the lattice produces chromatic fringing: a red fringe on one side of the edge and a blue fringe on the other, where the source image contained neither. On text it reads as colored speckle along stem edges — the classic "AMOLED text looks slightly rainbowy at small sizes" complaint.

Real panels never show the raw lattice, because the display driver runs subpixel rendering algorithms before the data reaches the emitters:

  • Grayscale antialiasing blurs edges in luminance only, then lets the panel resample — safe, but softer.
  • Subpixel-aware filtering treats each emitter as a spatially offset sample and re-distributes intensity across neighbouring subpixels, recovering edge sharpness at the cost of controlled, sub-visible fringing.
  • PenTile-aware compensation additionally corrects the shared-subpixel geometry: chroma is upsampled to the green grid before filtering, and glyph stems are slightly fattened so thin strokes survive the half-resolution chroma channels.

The simulator deliberately applies none of this — you are looking at the honest, uncompensated lattice, which is exactly what you want when judging how much margin a font weight, size or contrast ratio actually has. Emissive Panel Physics You Can Simulate

Turning "a grid of colored dots" into a believable panel means modelling how each emitter becomes light. The simulator's pipeline treats subpixels as physical sources, not filtered rectangles:

  • Linear-light pipeline. Color moves sRGB → linear → simulate → linear → sRGB. No additive math ever runs on gamma-encoded values.
  • Emitter response curve. Each emitter follows L = Iγ with its own drive gamma (default γ = 1.8), independent of the sRGB transfer function — the response of the organic material, not of the encoding.
  • Per-channel peak output. The channels are not equal citizens: red tops out near 0.70, green at 1.00, blue at 0.55 of normalized output. That skew is why raw, uncompensated regions can read greenish and why blue-heavy imagery is the hardest on a panel.
  • Optical spill. A microscopic Gaussian halo lets each emitter bleed a few percent of its energy into neighbours, with an independent spread σ per channel (green sharp, blue broad). Low spill shows the raw lattice; high spill optically re-mixes colors before your eye integrates the region.
  • Bloom. Separate from spill: a brightness-dependent extraction above a luminance threshold, blurred across the black matrix, so only genuine highlights halo — the glare you see staring at a bright widget on a dark screen.
  • Off-pixel state. Unlit subpixels are not perfectly sealed; a small off-level glow plus the lattice's black matrix defines contrast and true-black behaviour.

Every one of these is a live control in the simulator's Brightness panel — emitter γ, spill, per-channel σ, bloom floor and radius — so the section above is an invitation to move the sliders and watch the physics respond.

How to Test Text & UI Legibility on AMOLED

A practical workflow for verifying that a mobile app, website or design system survives a modern consumer panel:

  1. Capture at real density. Export a screenshot of your UI at the device resolution and pixel density of your target phone — not a Retina 2× mock, the actual raster.
  2. Load it into the simulator. Drop the file onto the simulator; media is decoded entirely in-tab, so screenshots with unreleased UI never leave your machine.
  3. Match the pitch. Set manual pitch to emulate the target panel, or keep auto density to inspect the lattice at a comfortable magnification.
  4. Stress the optics. Raise optical spill and bloom toward realistic levels and check small type: does the stem survive, do labels fringe, does critical information hold contrast next to a bright accent?
  5. Use the test pattern as a baseline. The built-in test pattern (diagonal, RGB bars, ring) shows pure grid behaviour — useful for an AMOLED screen test online before evaluating your own assets.
  6. Iterate on type. Bump weight, raise contrast, avoid the thinnest optical sizes, then re-run the same capture through the same settings.

AMOLED Subpixel FAQ

What is the Diamond PenTile subpixel layout?

The subpixel lattice used by most modern AMOLED phone panels: green emitters as small circles in a dense staggered checker, red and blue emitters as diamonds interleaved at half that density, with neighbouring pixels sharing subpixels — roughly two subpixels per pixel instead of the three of an RGB stripe pixel.

Why does AMOLED text have colored edges?

Chromatic fringing. Red and blue resolve at half horizontal resolution on a PenTile lattice with no aligned subpixel columns, so un-compensated sharp glyph edges pick up red and blue fringes. Subpixel rendering algorithms in the display driver compensate; heavier font weights reduce the visible artifact.

How do I run an AMOLED screen test online?

Open the simulator, use the built-in test pattern for grid behaviour, or drop in a screenshot of your own UI, set the subpixel pitch to a target panel density and raise optical spill and bloom to judge legibility, fringing and highlight halos at true reading distance.

What is the difference between spill and bloom on an OLED panel?

Optical spill is the microscopic Gaussian halo of each emitter bleeding a few percent of its energy into neighbouring subpixels before the eye integrates the region. Bloom is the macroscopic, brightness-dependent glow extracted above a luminance threshold, haloling highlights across the black matrix. The simulator keeps them on independent controls.

Does the simulator upload my screenshots anywhere?

No. px.ali.et is pure client-side with zero backend — images, GIFs and video are decoded and rendered inside your browser tab and never leave the device.
